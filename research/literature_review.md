## 1. Verification method and source policy

**Accepted as academic sources.** IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, Springer,
Elsevier/ScienceDirect, Wiley and peer-reviewed conferences. Blogs, Medium,
course notes and SEO content were *not* used as evidence for any claim.

**How each entry was checked.**

1. A DOI was proposed for the work.
2. The DOI was resolved against `https://api.crossref.org/works/{doi}`.
3. The returned **title was asserted against an expected fragment**. If the
   title did not match, the entry was *discarded*, not "corrected by hand".
4. Venue, publisher, year, volume, pages and type were taken from the Crossref
   record only — never from memory.
5. An independent index check was run against the **OpenAlex** API, which also
   reports whether the venue belongs to OpenAlex's curated *CORE* source set.

**This step was not a formality.** Of **40** DOIs put forward,
**4** did not resolve in Crossref at all, and **12
resolved to a different paper than the one intended**. Two real examples:
`10.1016/j.eswa.2018.06.039`, proposed for a utility-mining paper, is in fact
*"Resource2Vec: Linked Data distributed representations for term discovery in
automatic speech recognition"*; `10.1016/j.eswa.2015.06.009`, proposed for a
Wi-Fi fingerprinting paper, is in fact *"The facility layout problem in
non-rectangular logistics parks with split lines"*. Those entries were either
dropped or re-labelled to describe the work Crossref actually returned. The
surviving **24 entries** below are the ones whose Crossref title matched
the intended work. Had this check been skipped, roughly a third of the
bibliography would have cited papers that do not say what they were cited for.

### 1.1 Why no Q1/Q2 label is attached to anything here

Three rules were applied, in this order.

1. **A conference paper has no SJR/JCR quartile.** Quartiles rank *journals*
   (via SJR on Scopus data, or JIF on Web of Science data). CIKM, SIGKDD, IPIN,
   SIGMOD, INFOCOM, MobiSys and SDM papers in this review are therefore marked
   *"Not applicable"*, **not** Q1. The sentence "IEEE = Q1" is simply a category
   error: IEEE is a publisher, not a journal.
2. **For the journal articles, the ranking source was not reachable.**
   `scimagojr.com` returned **HTTP 403 Forbidden** from this environment on
   2026-10-07 — for the journal pages, for the ISSN search endpoint and for the
   bulk CSV export (`journalrank.php?out=xls`). No Scopus or Web of Science
   subscription is available here either.
3. Therefore every journal row says *"Not verified"* and gives the reason. A web
   search snippet did claim Q1 status for IEEE TKDE, but a search snippet is not
   a ranking source, so it is recorded here as hearsay and **not** entered into
   the table.

What *can* be stated factually: publisher, venue, year and DOI (Crossref), and
whether OpenAlex classifies the venue as a CORE source. Those columns are
populated; the quartile column is honest about being empty.

## 6. Thematic synthesis

### 6.1 From frequent itemsets to utility

[FACT FROM PAPER] Itemset mining begins with Agrawal, Imieliński and Swami
(SIGMOD 1993), who define items, itemsets, transactions and *support*, and pose
the problem of finding itemsets whose support passes a threshold. Han, Pei and
Yin (SIGMOD 2000) replace candidate generation with a *pattern-growth* search
over a compressed FP-tree.

[FACT FROM PAPER] Yao, Hamilton and Butz (SIAM SDM 2004) give the foundational
utility-mining formulation: each item carries an *external* utility (a per-item
weight such as unit profit) and an *internal* utility (a per-transaction
quantity), and an itemset's utility is the sum over transactions of the sum over
its items of the product. Crucially, they establish that this utility measure is
**neither monotone nor anti-monotone** with respect to itemset containment —
which is why the Apriori downward-closure trick does not transfer.

[FACT FROM PAPER] Liu, Liao and Choudhary (PAKDD 2005) resolve this with the
**Transaction-Weighted Utilization (TWU)** upper bound and a Two-Phase
algorithm: phase 1 mines itemsets whose TWU passes the threshold (TWU *is*
anti-monotone), phase 2 rescans the database to compute exact utilities and
filter. Gan et al. (TKDE 2021) and Fournier-Viger et al. (2019) both describe
this as the origin of essentially all later pruning.

### 6.2 The algorithmic line this project sits on

[FACT FROM PAPER] The weakness of Two-Phase is the size of the phase-1
candidate set. Two responses appear in the literature:

- **Reduce the candidates.** Tseng, Wu, Shie and Yu (SIGKDD 2010, journal
  version TKDE 2013) build an *UP-Tree* and apply DGU / DGN / DLU / DLN
  strategies to discard unpromising items during tree construction.
- **Remove the candidate phase entirely.** Liu and Qu (CIKM 2012) introduce
  **HUI-Miner** and the *utility-list*: for each itemset, a list of
  (tid, iutil, rutil) entries, where `iutil` is the utility of the itemset in
  that transaction and `rutil` the utility of the items that could still be
  appended. `iutil + rutil` summed over the list is an upper bound, so an
  itemset can be pruned without a second database pass. Fournier-Viger et al.
  (2014) add EUCP co-occurrence pruning on top (FHM).

[FACT FROM PAPER] Zida, Fournier-Viger, Lin, Wu and Tseng
(*Knowledge and Information Systems*, vol. 51, no. 2, pp. 595–625; DOI
`10.1007/s10115-016-0986-0`) present **EFIM**, which replaces utility-lists with
*high-utility database projection* and *transaction merging*, introduces two
tighter upper bounds — **local utility** and **subtree utility** — and computes
both in linear time via **Fast Utility Counting** over utility-bin arrays. The
paper reports EFIM as being up to orders of magnitude faster and using
substantially less memory than UP-Growth+, HUI-Miner, FHM and others.

### 6.3 Why EFIM is this project's primary algorithm — on evidence, not taste

[FACT FROM PAPER] Zhang, Almpanidis, Wang and Liu (*Expert Systems with
Applications*, vol. 101, pp. 91–115, 2018; DOI
`10.1016/j.eswa.2018.02.008`) is an **independent, third-party** empirical
evaluation of HUIM algorithms — i.e. not written by EFIM's authors. Relying on
an independent benchmark rather than on the proposing paper's own experiments is
the reason EFIM, and not HUI-Miner or UP-Growth, is implemented here.

[OUR INTERPRETATION] Two properties of EFIM matter specifically for
UJIIndoorLoc rather than for market-basket data. First, transaction merging pays
off when many transactions are identical after projection; our audit found
**852 training rows whose 520-column WAP vector
duplicates another row's**, and sparsity means projected transactions collide
often. Second, EFIM's memory profile matters because the database here is wide
(520 items) rather than deep.

### 6.4 Wi-Fi fingerprint indoor positioning and UJIIndoorLoc

[FACT FROM PAPER] Bahl and Padmanabhan (INFOCOM 2000, RADAR) establish the
fingerprinting paradigm: record RSSI vectors at known locations *offline*, then
*online* match a new vector to the stored ones by nearest-neighbour search in
signal space. Youssef and Agrawala (MobiSys 2005, Horus) give the probabilistic
alternative. Kaemarungsi and Krishnamurthy (INFOCOM 2004) analyse fingerprint
properties and show that APs differ in how much they contribute.
Khalajmehrabadi, Gatsis and Akopian (*IEEE Communications Surveys & Tutorials*,
2017) survey the field and identify fingerprint dimensionality as a deployment
problem in its own right.

[FACT FROM PAPER] Torres-Sospedra et al. (IPIN 2014, DOI
`10.1109/IPIN.2014.7275492`) publish **UJIIndoorLoc**: a multi-building,
multi-floor WLAN fingerprint database, described in the abstract as *"the
biggest database in the literature"* and *"the first publicly available
database"* of its kind, with raw data from more than 20 users and 25 devices.

[FACT FROM PAPER] Torres-Sospedra et al. (*Expert Systems with Applications*,
vol. 42, pp. 9263–9278, 2015) evaluate 51 distance metrics and 4 RSSI
representations on UJIIndoorLoc. [OUR INTERPRETATION] This is why the baseline
classifier in this study is k-NN with an explicitly documented RSSI
representation: it is the configuration the UJIIndoorLoc literature itself
treats as the reference point, so a comparison against it is meaningful.

### 6.5 Access-point selection is established prior art

This matters for stating our contribution honestly.

[FACT FROM PAPER] Chen, Yang, Yin et al. (*IEEE TKDE*, vol. 18, pp. 877–888,
2006) propose power-efficient **access-point selection** for indoor location
estimation. Song, Wang, Yuan et al. (*ISPRS IJGI*, vol. 5, 189, 2016) perform
fingerprint localization with **best-discriminating AP selection**. Lohan,
Torres-Sospedra et al. (*Data*, vol. 2, 32, 2017) show that the RSSI
representation itself (e.g. powed / exponential) changes positioning results.

[OUR INTERPRETATION] Therefore "selecting a subset of APs", and even "selecting
APs by a discriminative information-theoretic criterion", are **not** novel
contributions of this project. What has not been located in the literature is
the use of a *high-utility itemset miner* as the selection mechanism — i.e.
selecting APs because they participate in itemsets whose aggregate utility
passes a threshold, rather than scoring each AP independently.

## 7. The existence question: has HUIM been applied to UJIIndoorLoc / Wi-Fi IPS?

Searches executed (all on 2026-10-07):

| Query | Outcome |
|---|---|
| `"high utility itemset mining" indoor positioning Wi-Fi fingerprint` | No work combining the two. Results were either Wi-Fi fingerprinting (LightGBM, k-NN, Bayesian) or HUIM, never both. |
| `"high utility itemset" UJIIndoorLoc access point selection` | Returned HUIM papers and UJIIndoorLoc papers as **separate** literatures. |
| `utility mining / "utility itemset" applied indoor localization RSSI access point feature selection pattern mining` | Returned Wi-Fi feature-selection work (DMD, HMM) and utility-mining work separately; no intersection. |

Searches were also run across Crossref and OpenAlex for the seed works; no
citing or cited work in the retrieved metadata links EFIM-family HUIM to WLAN
fingerprint localisation.

> **Required statement.** Trong phạm vi literature review đã kiểm chứng, chưa
> tìm thấy bằng chứng đủ mạnh để coi ánh xạ HUIM → UJIIndoorLoc dưới đây là
> phương pháp chuẩn hóa đã được công nhận; do đó phần này được xem là mô hình áp
> dụng/đề xuất thực nghiệm của đồ án.

**What this statement does and does not claim.** It claims that *this* review,
with *these* queries and *these* accessible databases, found no such work. It
does **not** claim that no such work exists. IEEE Xplore and the ACM DL
full-text search were not reachable from this environment, and absence of
evidence in a non-systematic review is not evidence of absence. No novelty
priority is asserted anywhere in this project.

## 8. Gap and positioning of this study

| | |
|---|---|
| **Established in the literature** | HUIM formalism and algorithms (EFIM); UJIIndoorLoc as a benchmark; k-NN fingerprint matching; AP selection, including discriminative/information-theoretic AP selection. |
| **Not found in the literature** | HUIM used as the AP-subset selection mechanism for Wi-Fi fingerprint localisation; any utility definition mapping RSSI to internal utility and AP discriminativeness to external utility. |
| **What this project therefore is** | "Đồ án khảo sát khả năng thích nghi mô hình High Utility Itemset Mining cho bài toán lựa chọn nhóm Access Point trong Wi-Fi fingerprint indoor positioning." An *adaptation study* with an ablation over utility definitions — not a claim of a new algorithm, and not a claim of priority. |
| **What is borrowed vs. designed** | *Borrowed (literature):* the HUIM problem definition, TWU, EFIM's bounds and data structures, the k-NN baseline, the use of mutual information as a discriminativeness measure. *Designed here (ours):* the fingerprint→transaction encoding, the two utility models, and the HUI→AP-subset ranking rule. |
