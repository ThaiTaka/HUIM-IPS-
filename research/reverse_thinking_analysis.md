### OBSERVATION

[EXPERIMENT RESULT] Model A (signal utility): dividing the threshold by 1.1547 took the output from 731 to 593,823 itemsets. The number of items surviving the root TWU bound moved only from 258 to 263, and the number of search-tree nodes visited from 8,550,400 to 15,675,392.

[EXPERIMENT RESULT] Model B (discriminative utility): dividing the threshold by 1.3333 took the output from 24,621 to 1,000,001 itemsets. The number of items surviving the root TWU bound moved only from 204 to 235, and the number of search-tree nodes visited from 224,711 to 1,912,614.

[EXPERIMENT RESULT] Model C (rare-discriminative utility): dividing the threshold by 1.1547 took the output from 52,902 to 1,000,001 itemsets. The number of items surviving the root TWU bound moved only from 245 to 259, and the number of search-tree nodes visited from 348,659 to 3,134,917.

### WHY?

The threshold moved by a small multiplicative factor; the output moved by orders of magnitude. The explanation is not in the threshold, it is in the shape of the search space.

[FACT FROM DATA] A fingerprint detects on average 17.995 access points. Those APs are not independent: they are the APs installed near one physical location, so they are seen together over and over.

[OUR INTERPRETATION] Suppose a group of *k* APs co-occurs in many fingerprints with high signal. Then **every one of its 2^k − 1 non-empty subsets also co-occurs at least as often**, and each subset's utility is a partial sum of the same per-transaction utilities. Once the threshold drops below the level where the *shortest* of those subsets qualifies, the whole subset lattice qualifies at once. The output does not grow item by item; it grows by whole power sets.

This is visible in the measured lengths: 
Model A (signal utility) at 1.9294% produced 853 HUIs drawn from only 86 distinct WAPs; Model B (discriminative utility) at 2% produced 24,621 HUIs drawn from only 79 distinct WAPs; Model C (rare-discriminative utility) at 1.7321% produced 52,902 HUIs drawn from only 91 distinct WAPs.

[OUR INTERPRETATION] That ratio is the signature of the mechanism: huge numbers of itemsets over a small alphabet means the itemsets are subsets of each other, not independent discoveries. **A million HUIs here is not a million findings; it is a handful of AP groups counted once per subset.**

### ALGORITHM MECHANISM

[FACT FROM PAPER] EFIM prunes with two upper bounds (Zida et al. 2017): *local utility* removes an item from an entire subtree, and *subtree utility* prevents a branch from being entered at all. Both are bounds on what the **best possible descendant** could achieve.

[OUR INTERPRETATION] A bound can only prune a branch that cannot contain an answer. When the threshold falls, branches that genuinely *do* contain answers stop being prunable — correctly. EFIM is not failing at low minUtil; it is being asked to enumerate an output that is itself exponential. No pruning rule can shrink a correct answer set.

Measured confirmation that the items themselves are not the problem: the root TWU bound keeps only 
17–263 of 465 items (Model A (signal utility)), 41–235 of 465 items (Model B (discriminative utility)), 31–259 of 465 items (Model C (rare-discriminative utility)). A modest change in the surviving alphabet accompanies an enormous change in the output, so the explosion is combinatorial in the *depth* of the search, not in its *breadth*.

### HUIM THEORY

[FACT FROM PAPER] Utility is neither monotone nor anti-monotone (Yao et al. 2004). Support has downward closure — a superset can never be more frequent than its subset — and that single property is what keeps frequent-itemset mining tractable. Utility has no such property: adding an item adds its utility (pushing up) while possibly shrinking the set of transactions that contain the itemset (pushing down).

[FACT FROM PAPER] Because of that, pruning must use a *surrogate* that does have the property. TWU is anti-monotone (Liu et al. 2005); local and subtree utility are tighter versions of the same idea. All of them are **over-estimates**, so they are safe, but they are also **thresholds on a bound, not on the answer** — which is why their effectiveness collapses exactly when the threshold stops separating promising from unpromising branches.

### IPS MEANING

[OUR INTERPRETATION] For positioning, this settles how minUtil should be read. minUtil is **not** an accuracy knob and **not** a quality knob — it is the knob that decides *how large a group of co-occurring APs counts as a pattern*. A high threshold describes only the densest AP clusters on the campus. A low threshold describes the same clusters plus every sub-combination of them, which is more output carrying almost no extra information. For an AP-selection application, the useful operating point is therefore near the *top* of the threshold range, not the bottom — the opposite of the instinct that 'more patterns must be better'.

### OBSERVATION

[EXPERIMENT RESULT] Runtime spans 0.10–600.89 s (Model A (signal utility)), 0.23–23.29 s (Model B (discriminative utility)), 0.19–99.17 s (Model C (rare-discriminative utility)). The rank correlation between nodes visited and runtime is 0.995, 0.973, 0.998 respectively.

### WHY?

[OUR INTERPRETATION] A near-perfect rank correlation between nodes visited and runtime means the cost model is simply *per node*: each node costs one projection of the current database plus one Fast Utility Counting pass. Lowering the threshold does not make a node more expensive; it makes there be more nodes. So runtime is a measure of how much of the lattice EFIM was forced to walk.

Memory behaves differently from runtime, and the reason is worth separating: EFIM's *working set* is small (one projected database per recursion level, and transaction merging keeps those small), but the *result list* grows with the number of HUIs found. Peak memory therefore tracks output size rather than search size.

### ALGORITHM MECHANISM

[FACT FROM PAPER] EFIM's memory design is deliberate: high-utility database projection reuses the parent's arrays rather than building utility-lists per itemset (the cost that dominates HUI-Miner), and transaction merging fuses projected transactions that have become identical.

[FACT FROM DATA] Measured merging in this database: up to 76,702,637 transactions fused (Model A (signal utility)); up to 5,541,005 transactions fused (Model B (discriminative utility)); up to 7,022,431 transactions fused (Model C (rare-discriminative utility)).

[OUR INTERPRETATION] Merging works here for a reason specific to Wi-Fi data. Projection repeatedly strips the leading APs off each transaction; what is left are the tails. Fingerprints taken at nearby points have very similar tails, so after a few projections many transactions become literally identical and collapse into one. A retail basket database, where baskets are idiosyncratic, benefits much less.

### HUIM THEORY

[FACT FROM PAPER] This is the distinction the independent benchmark of Zhang et al. (ESWA 2018) makes between algorithms: one-phase methods removed the candidate-generation memory cost, and EFIM additionally removed the per-itemset data-structure cost. What *cannot* be removed is the cost of writing down an exponentially large correct answer.

### IPS MEANING

[OUR INTERPRETATION] For a deployed positioning system the practical consequence is that HUIM belongs in the **offline** phase, and its cost must be budgeted as a function of the threshold, not of the database size. Mining is a one-off site-survey computation; the online phase only ever sees the resulting AP list, which costs nothing. A system designer needs the threshold-vs-runtime curve measured here, not an average runtime.

[EXPERIMENT RESULT] A_signal vs C_rare_discriminative: Jaccard 0.475, with 0 of the top 10 shared.

[EXPERIMENT RESULT] B_discriminative vs C_rare_discriminative: Jaccard 0.868, with 6 of the top 10 shared.

### WHY?

[OUR INTERPRETATION] The two models share the same internal utility and differ only in p(i), so any difference is attributable entirely to the external utility. Model A's p ≡ 1 makes utility a function of *signal strength × how often the APs are seen together*: it rewards APs that are strong and ubiquitous. Model B multiplies by the AP's mutual information with the floor label, which *down-weights exactly those ubiquitous APs*, because an AP visible everywhere tells you nothing about where you are.

### ALGORITHM MECHANISM

[OUR INTERPRETATION] The effect runs through the bounds, not just through the final utilities. TWU(i) = Σ_{T ∋ i} TU(T) is computed from the *same* p values, so changing p changes which items survive the root bound, which changes the item ordering (EFIM sorts by increasing TWU), which changes the shape of the entire search tree. Two utility definitions therefore do not merely re-score the same patterns; they explore different trees and can return structurally different patterns.

### HUIM THEORY

[FACT FROM PAPER] This is the property that makes utility mining more than weighted frequent-itemset mining (Gan et al., TKDE 2021): the utility function is part of the problem statement, not a post-hoc ranking. [OUR INTERPRETATION] For an applied study the corollary is uncomfortable but important — **'the high utility itemsets of this dataset' is not a well-defined object.** It is only defined relative to a (q, p) choice that the analyst makes.

### IPS MEANING

[OUR INTERPRETATION] If the goal is localisation, the utility must encode something about localisation. Model A's notion of 'useful' — strong and everywhere — is close to the *opposite* of what a fingerprint needs, which is an AP whose visibility pattern varies between places. Model B was designed to encode that, and the overlap figures above show the design does change the answer rather than merely re-labelling it.

Building accuracy survives aggressive reduction because a handful of building-exclusive APs carry it. Floor accuracy is the binding constraint. A report that quoted only building accuracy would make the method look far better than it is.

**Read this carefully, because it does not say what the slogan 'frequent is not useful' would lead one to expect.** The two criteria are *positively* associated, and at the very top of the ranking they largely agree. On this dataset, an AP that is detected often also tends to carry information — which makes sense: an AP detected twice cannot distinguish anything, so some coverage is a precondition for informativeness.

[OUR INTERPRETATION] So Observation 6 does **not** establish 'frequent ≠ useful' on this data. What it establishes is weaker and more precise: the two orderings are correlated but not identical, and the disagreement grows as one goes deeper into the ranking. The decisive evidence that signal-driven utility picks *bad* features is not here at all — it is in Observation 5 and Observation 9, where Model A's ranking is measured against chance.

[FACT FROM DATA] The label entropy H(Y) of the 13 BUILDINGID_FLOOR classes is 3.63916 bits. The single most informative AP supplies 0.43812 bits, i.e. 12.0% of it.

### WHY?

[OUR INTERPRETATION] Mutual information with the location label is maximised by an AP whose *state varies with location*. Coverage and informativeness therefore pull in the same direction up to a point — an AP nobody ever sees cannot discriminate — and then separate: an AP visible across several floors has support but little information, while an AP seen in a few hundred fingerprints all on one floor is almost a floor detector. That is exactly the shape the rank-overlap figures above show: agreement at the top, divergence with depth.

[FACT FROM DATA] The structural reason is in the audit: 323 of the detected APs are confined to one building while 139 are seen in two and 3 in all three. Confinement is what creates information.

### HUIM THEORY

[FACT FROM PAPER] The motivation for utility mining over frequent-itemset mining (Yao et al. 2004; Gan et al. 2021) is that frequency and worth are different axes: an item can be rare and valuable or common and worthless.

[OUR INTERPRETATION] On this dataset that separation is real but *partial* — the measured association above is positive. The place where the distinction bites hardest is not single-item frequency at all, but **Model A's utility**, which multiplies signal strength by how often APs co-occur, and whose ranking performs worse than chance (Observation 9). That is the clean instance of 'what looks valuable is not what is useful', and it is what makes Model A a meaningful control rather than a wasted arm of the ablation.

### IPS MEANING

[OUR INTERPRETATION] The practical lesson for a site survey is narrower than the slogan: do not keep APs because their *signal is strong*. Keeping APs because they are *widely detected* is, on this data, not a bad heuristic at all — the top-n-by-detection-count control is competitive throughout, and at 200 APs it is the best condition in the whole study. What a fingerprint database needs is coverage first, and then boundaries that cut through the building.

## Observation 7 — Why thresholds must be relative, not absolute

### RESULT

| Model | Total database utility | Mean TU(T) | Max TU(T) | p range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Model A (signal utility) | 9,058,242.00 | 456.08 | 2,173.00 | 1 – 1 |
| Model B (discriminative utility) | 4,354,299.06 | 219.24 | 772.96 | 1e-06 – 1 |
| Model C (rare-discriminative utility) | 6,211,852.27 | 312.77 | 965.28 | 1e-06 – 1 |

### OBSERVATION / WHY?

[EXPERIMENT RESULT] Total database utility differs by a factor of 2.08 between the models, even though the database, the transactions and the internal utilities are identical. Only p changed.

[OUR INTERPRETATION] An absolute minUtil of, say, 200,000 is therefore meaningless as a cross-model setting: the same number sits at completely different points of the three utility distributions. Expressing minUtil as a fraction of total database utility is what makes the three arms of the ablation comparable at all, and it is also what let the same grid be reused between the pilot subsample and the full database.

[FACT FROM PAPER] This is standard practice in the HUIM literature, where thresholds are normally reported as a percentage of total utility precisely because absolute utility has no interpretation across datasets (Zhang et al., ESWA 2018).

## Observation 8 — Do more HUIs mean better positioning?

### RESULT

- Union conditions evaluated: 19
- Spearman ρ(number of HUIs, joint accuracy) = **0.6254**
- Spearman ρ(number of selected WAPs, joint accuracy) = **0.9126**
- Spearman ρ(number of selected WAPs, joint accuracy) over the top-n sweep = **0.8643**

[OUR INTERPRETATION] union points vary minUtil, so n_huis and n_waps move together; the top-n sweep separates them. A correlation computed over union points cannot separate 'more itemsets' from 'more features', because lowering minUtil increases both at once. The top-n sweep holds the HUI set fixed and varies only the budget, which is the comparison that isolates the feature count.

### ANSWER

[OUR INTERPRETATION] The question 'does a larger HUI set give better accuracy' is the wrong question, and Observation 1 explains why: a larger HUI set at lower minUtil is mostly **additional subsets of the same AP groups**, so it adds itemsets without adding APs. What changes accuracy is which APs end up in the union, not how many itemsets mentioned them. Any claim of the form 'mining more patterns improves localisation' is therefore unsupported by this experiment.

### WHY? / INTERPRETATION

[OUR INTERPRETATION] These numbers separate two explanations that are easy to confuse.

**Explanation 1 — the ranking is wrong.** If HUIM's ordering of APs were no better than chance, it would lose to random selection at matched budgets. The table above answers that per model, and the answer differs by model — which is itself the point of the ablation.

**Explanation 2 — the set is too small.** HUIM can only offer the APs that appear in a non-truncated HUI set, and that count is capped by the lowest minUtil that still completes inside the resource budget. Here that ceiling is 91 WAPs, while the simplest control needs 200 to match the baseline. **Even a perfect ranking could not have closed the gap from inside that ceiling.**

[OUR INTERPRETATION] This is the most useful mechanistic finding of the study, and it points straight back at Observation 1. To enlarge the union, minUtil must fall; but lowering minUtil multiplies the *itemsets* far faster than it adds *APs*, and the mining cost explodes before the AP set grows usefully. The feature-set size a practitioner needs and the threshold region HUIM can actually be run in are, on this dataset and with these utility definitions, pulling in opposite directions.

[OUR INTERPRETATION] Note what this does *not* say. It does not say HUIM's ranking is bad — that is the separate question the table above addresses. It says that the operating point where HUIM-selected features could compete was not reachable within the compute budget used here, which is a statement about this experiment's design as much as about the method.

## What the experiments force us to conclude about HUIM itself

Reading the chain backwards, from results to theory:

1. [EXPERIMENT RESULT] Output size is extremely sensitive to minUtil, while the number of distinct APs involved is not. → [THEORY] Because utility lacks downward closure, the answer set is a union of subset lattices, and its size is governed by the depth of the deepest qualifying itemset.

2. [EXPERIMENT RESULT] Runtime scales with nodes visited, memory with output size. → [THEORY] EFIM's bounds control *search*, not *output*. No correct algorithm can do better on output.

3. [EXPERIMENT RESULT] Changing only p changes which APs are selected. → [THEORY] The utility function is part of the problem definition, so 'the HUIs of a dataset' is not well defined without it.

4. [EXPERIMENT RESULT] Detection count and mutual information turned out to be almost perfectly rank-correlated here, so 'frequent ≠ useful' was NOT demonstrated at the level of individual APs — the opposite of what this study expected to find. → [THEORY] The distinction still holds, but it bit at the level of the utility FUNCTION rather than the item: Model A, whose p ≡ 1 makes utility a product of signal strength and co-occurrence, ranked APs worse than chance.

5. [THEORY → PRACTICE] HUIM is not a supervised feature selector. Any usefulness it has for localisation comes from the utility definition that the analyst supplies, and must be demonstrated against controls rather than assumed.


### Explicitly NOT claimed

- That a high-utility itemset is, in general, a good feature group for classification.

- That HUIM is a better AP-selection method than the existing information-theoretic ones; the matched-budget table is the only evidence offered, and it is read as it stands.

- That these results generalise beyond UJIIndoorLoc, one k-NN configuration, and the (q, p) definitions used here.

- Any novelty priority. See `research/literature_review.md` §7.
